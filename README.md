<h1><strong>14 Tips for Test Taking Success Backed by Research</strong></h1>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Test taking success is part preparation, part strategy, and part keeping your nerves from hijacking what you already know.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Whether you are facing a college final, a professional license exam, or a high-stakes admissions test, the same habits keep showing up in the research:&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Study in spaced sessions, practice pulling answers out of memory, sleep enough, and run a calm plan once the clock starts.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">These 14 tips are organized in the order you will actually use them, from weeks before the exam to the final minutes at your desk.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Pick the ones that fit your situation and start with the first three, since they do the most work.</span></p>
<h2><strong>Before the Exam: Build Your Knowledge</strong></h2>
<h3><strong>1. Spread Your Studying Over Time</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Cramming feels productive because the material is fresh in your head. The trouble is that it fades quickly and piles on stress.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Spaced practice, which means studying the same content on different days, helps you hold onto it longer.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Psychologists such as John Dunlosky and colleagues have identified it as a key component of doing well on exams, as summarized in SAGE's student success guide.</span></p>
<p><strong>Do this:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Break each topic into short sessions across several days or weeks. Review old material briefly each time before starting new material.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">This matters most for exams that cover a lot of ground over many months.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Candidates sitting ACCA papers, for example, cannot memorize an entire syllabus in the last week, so a steady schedule beats a heroic final push.</span></p>

<h3><strong>2. Test Yourself Instead of Rereading</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Rereading creates a comfortable feeling of familiarity, which is not the same as knowing something.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Retrieval practice, the habit of pulling answers from memory, is a stronger approach. Bryn Mawr College's academic support office notes that practice tests have been found to work better than rereading notes, and that students who actively practiced retrieval learned more than students who reread.</span></p>
<p><strong>Do this:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Close the book and write down everything you remember about a topic. Use flashcards, make your own quiz questions, or turn each heading in your notes into a question.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">If testing sounds like it might make you more nervous, the research points the other way.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">In a survey of 1,500 students reported by retrieval practice, 72% said weekly retrieval practice decreased their test anxiety.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">That is self-reported data, but it fits the logic: the more often you check what you know, the less you have to guess.</span></p>
<h3><strong>3. Take Full-Length Practice Exams Under Real Conditions</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A practice test teaches you two things: what you know, and how you perform when the clock is running.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Do at least a few under realistic conditions, with the same time limit, no phone, and no pausing to look things up.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Licensing exams make this especially useful. Kaplan Financial Education's insurance licensing courses, for instance, include a Mastery Exam designed to replicate the state exam experience, so you can gauge readiness before test day.&nbsp;</span></p>

<p><strong>Do this:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Schedule your practice exams at the same time of day as the real one, and set a pass or readiness target before you start.</span></p>
<h3><strong>4. Learn the Format, Rules, and Scoring</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">You do not want surprises on exam day. Find out how many questions there are, what types appear, whether wrong answers lose points, whether you can skip and return, and what is allowed in the room.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Read the candidate handbook or the test sponsor's instructions at least a week early.</span></p>
<p><strong>Do this:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Write a one-page summary of the format and keep it with your study materials. If you will test online or at a center, learn the check-in process too.</span></p>
<h3><strong>5. Plan Backward From the Test Date</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A study plan removes daily decision fatigue. Start with the exam date and work backward, assigning topics to specific days and leaving the final week for review and practice tests.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Working adults often juggle study time with a job, which makes planning even more important.&nbsp;</span></p>

<p><strong>Do this:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Block study sessions on your calendar like appointments, and keep them short enough to repeat: 30 to 90 minutes works for most people.</span></p>
<h3><strong>6. Keep an Error Log</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Every wrong practice answer is free information. Instead of just checking the correct answer and moving on, write down why you missed it:&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A knowledge gap, a careless mistake, a misread question, or poor time use.</span></p>
<p><strong>Do this:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Keep a simple table with the question, the reason you missed it, and the fix. Review the log once a week.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Patterns will jump out fast, and they tell you where your next study hour should go.</span></p>
<h2><strong>The Week and Night Before</strong></h2>
<h3><strong>7. Protect Your Sleep</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Sleep is where memory gets stored, and a short night can undo days of studying.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Sleep medicine groups recommend that adults get seven or more hours per night, and teens need 8 to 10 hours, according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine's Sleep Education site. The CDC publishes similar age-based guidance.</span></p>
<p><strong>Do this:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Keep a consistent bedtime for the week leading up to the exam. If nerves keep you up the night before, do not panic. Good sleep in the days before counts too.</span></p>
<h3><strong>8. Eat, Hydrate, and Move</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Your brain is part of your body, and it does worse on an empty stomach or a caffeine overload. Aim for a balanced breakfast on test day, drink water, and avoid trying a new energy drink or heavy meal for the first time that morning.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A short walk the day before can burn off nervous energy.</span></p>
<p><strong>Do this:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Pack a snack and water if the rules allow, and stick to foods you already know sit well with you.</span></p>
<h3><strong>9. Handle the Logistics Early</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Many avoidable disasters have nothing to do with knowledge: a missing ID, a late arrival, a laptop that will not connect. Prepare everything the day before.</span></p>

<h2><strong>During the Exam: Execute Your Plan</strong></h2>
<h3><strong>10. Calm Your Nerves With a Simple Routine</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Some stress is normal and can even help. Research summarized by Bryn Mawr's academic support office found that students taught to view stress as helping performance did better under stress.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The goal is not to erase nerves, but to keep them from crowding out thinking.</span></p>
<p><strong>Do this:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> When you sit down, take three slow breaths, with a long exhale. Remind yourself of one fact: "I have practiced this."</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">If your mind blanks, pause, breathe, and move to a question you can answer.</span></p>
<h3><strong>11. Scan the Test and Budget Your Time</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Spend the first minute reading all directions and scanning the whole exam. Decide how much time each section deserves, and note halfway checkpoints.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Pacing matters most on long exams. A test like the MCAT takes most of a workday, so stamina is part of the skill.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">If that is on your calendar, check the</span><a href="https://kaplan.discount/mcat/"> <span style="font-weight: 400;">Kaplan MCAT promo code</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> page for prep packages that include full-length practice, and verify the current terms.</span></p>
<p><strong>Do this:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Write your time checkpoints at the top of the page (for example, "25 questions by minute 30") and glance at the clock only at those points.</span></p>
<h3><strong>12. Make Multiple Passes</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Do not get stuck wrestling with one stubborn question while easy points wait. Bryn Mawr's guidance notes that research has shown multiple passes through a test lead to better results.</span></p>
<p><strong>Do this:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Answer everything you know right away.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Mark the tough ones and move on.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Return to the marked questions with the time left.</span></li>
</ol>
<h3><strong>13. Read Carefully and Eliminate Wrong Answers</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Many missed questions come from misreading, not missing knowledge. Underline key words like "not," "except," and "most," and answer in your own words before looking at the options.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">When you are unsure, eliminate answers you know are wrong, then pick from what is left.</span></p>
<p><strong>Do this:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Check the scoring rules first. If wrong answers carry no penalty, never leave a question blank.</span></p>
<h3><strong>14. Review Your Answers, Then Learn From the Result</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">If time remains, use it. Check for skipped questions, mismarked answer sheets, and simple arithmetic slips.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Be cautious about changing answers unless you can name a specific reason, such as a misread word or a recalculated result.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">After the exam, take ten minutes to write down what worked and what did not. If you get your results back, add any missed topics to your error log.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">That feedback becomes your head start for the next exam.</span></p>
<h2><strong>Common Test Taking Mistakes to Avoid</strong></h2>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Cramming all night.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> It raises stress and costs you sleep.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Studying only what feels comfortable.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Your weakest topics deserve the most time.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Skipping practice exams.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Familiarity with the format saves real minutes.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Comparing notes in the hallway.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Post-exam postmortems can spike anxiety before your next exam.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Ignoring the instructions.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Read them every time, even if you think you know them.</span></li>
</ul>

<h2><strong>Frequently Asked Questions</strong></h2>
<h3><strong>What is the best way to prepare for a test?</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Combine spaced practice and retrieval practice: study in short sessions across multiple days, and test yourself instead of rereading. Add timed practice exams as the date gets closer.</span></p>
<h3><strong>How many hours of sleep do I need before an exam?</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Sleep medicine groups recommend 7 or more hours for adults and 8 to 10 hours for teens. Aim for consistent sleep all week, not only the night before.</span></p>
<h3><strong>How do I stop test anxiety?</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">You may not eliminate it, but you can manage it. Practice under realistic conditions, use slow breathing, and prepare so well that fewer surprises remain. If anxiety is severe or interferes with your life, talk with a counselor or health professional.</span></p>
<h3><strong>Should I change my answers when I review?</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Only when you have a clear reason, such as a misread word or a recalculated figure. Do not change answers just because of a vague feeling.</span></p>
<h3><strong>Is it better to guess or leave questions blank?</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Check the scoring rules. If wrong answers are not penalized, guess. If they are, make educated guesses only when you can eliminate at least one option.</span></p>
<h2><strong>Final Thoughts</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Strong test takers are not magic. They start early, practice recalling information, protect their sleep, and follow a plan under pressure. Start with spaced practice and weekly timed tests, build in rest, and walk in with a pacing plan. Do those things consistently and the exam stops feeling like a mystery and starts feeling like a rehearsal.</span></p>
